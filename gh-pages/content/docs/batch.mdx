title: Batch optimize

<Key>Ctrl+B</Key> opens the batch panel. It walks three steps — select, settings,
run — and writes its results into a new folder, leaving your originals alone
unless you explicitly ask otherwise.

## 1 · Select

Pick which files take part. Videos and photos are counted separately, so you can
re-encode a folder's clips and leave its photos untouched.

## 2 · Settings

<Shot
  src="/screenshots/batch-settings.png"
  alt="Batch settings: codec, quality, resolution, audio and output folder"
  caption="Defaults: H.265 at CRF 28, capped to 1080p, AAC audio at 128 kbps."
  width={2200}
  height={1514}
/>

Five presets sit at the top — *Max savings · 1080p H.265*, *Balanced · H.264*,
*Fast remux · no quality loss*, *HEIC → JPEG 90* and *Web · WebP 2560px* — and
you can save your own settings as a named preset next to them.

**Video.** H.265, H.264 or AV1, plus a remux-only *stream copy* if all you want
is a faster-starting container. Quality is a CRF value (lower is bigger and
better), and you can cap the height and the frame rate.

**Photos.** JPEG, WebP, AVIF or PNG, at a quality of your choosing, optionally
resized by longest edge. *Keep* re-encodes without changing the format.

**Output.** By default everything lands in `_optimized/` next to the source. You
can also send it all to one folder you choose, or replace the originals — that
last one needs a separate confirmation and keeps a backup unless you turn the
backup off.

**Safety valve.** Files whose converted version comes out larger than the
original are skipped and reported as skipped, rather than quietly making your
folder bigger.

## The size estimate

Before the run starts, the app encodes a handful of real samples with your exact
settings: up to three three-second slices of video, and up to five of the photos.
The predicted output size is extrapolated from those. It is a measurement of
your files, not a lookup table, which is why it is worth the few seconds it
takes.

## Hardware encoding

With **Auto** acceleration the app probes the machine and uses NVENC, Quick
Sync, AMF, VideoToolbox or VAAPI when the device and the FFmpeg build both
support the codec you picked. You can also pin a specific backend, or force
software encoding.

<Callout title="A failed GPU job is not a failed batch">
If a hardware encode fails partway, that file is retried on the CPU and the
fallback is recorded in the report. You end up with the file either way, and you
can see which ones took the slow path.
</Callout>

## Running, and surviving a crash

<Shot
  src="/screenshots/batch-progress.png"

Every completed file is checkpointed. If the app closes mid-run — crash, reboot,
impatience — the next launch keeps the finished results, deletes the incomplete
temporary output, and restarts only what was still pending.

failed files are named rather than summarised, so you can go and look at them.

## Dates and EXIF

Batch conversion is the one place the app rewrites file contents, so the EXIF
block is copied across explicitly. That works for JPEG, PNG and WebP output.
AVIF has nowhere to put it, and the settings panel says so before you start.
File timestamps are reapplied to the converted copy either way.

Batch optimize requires FFmpeg. On the Lite build, [install it
first](/docs/install).
